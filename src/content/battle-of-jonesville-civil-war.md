# The Battle of Jonesville: The Frozen Fight That Captured 383 Union Soldiers in Lee County, Virginia

![The Dickinson-Milbourn House, a surviving landmark of the Battle of Jonesville](Dickison.webp)

*On a bitterly cold January morning in 1864, Confederate cavalry descended on Jonesville, Virginia. By sunset, a Union command had been overwhelmed, hundreds of soldiers were prisoners, and an imposing brick house on the edge of town had become part of a battlefield. The building is still standing.*

The **Battle of Jonesville**, fought **January 2–3, 1864**, is one of Southwest Virginia's most consequential but frequently overlooked Civil War engagements. Confederate Brigadier General William E. “Grumble” Jones aimed to threaten the Union-held **Cumberland Gap**. Instead, his troops encountered a Union cavalry force in the Lee County seat of Jonesville. The clash ended with the surrender of Union Major Charles H. Beeres's command and the reported capture of **383 Union officers and men**.

This is not simply a story about troop movements in the mountains. At its center stands the **Dickinson-Milbourn House**, a brick residence built before the war. Union soldiers sheltered around the house and its outbuildings as the fighting intensified. The battlefield itself was not preserved as a protected park, but the house, markers, historic records and landscape preserve pieces of the story for visitors today.

The central facts are documented in the armies' *Official Records*, the [Virginia Tech Civil War Studies battle account](https://civilwar.vt.edu/programs/drivingtour/battleofjonesville.html), and the [Virginia Department of Historic Resources' Dickinson-Milbourn House listing](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/historic-registers/245-0004/).

## TL;DR

- **Where:** Jonesville, county seat of Lee County, in far Southwest Virginia.
- **When:** January 2–3, **1864**; the major Confederate assault and surrender occurred January 3.
- **Who fought:** Confederate cavalry under Brig. Gen. **William E. “Grumble” Jones**, supported by Lt. Col. **Auburn L. Pridemore**, against a Union force led by Maj. **Charles H. Beeres**, primarily associated with the **16th Illinois Cavalry** and supporting artillery.
- **Outcome:** Confederate victory; Jones officially reported **383 captured**, **45 of those wounded**, **10 Union dead**, **three artillery pieces**, and **27 six-mule wagons and teams** taken. These are figures reported by a participant, not an independently audited modern casualty roll.
- **Key historic site:** The **Dickinson-Milbourn House**, where Union troops took cover before the surrender. It is a **private residence**, not a public museum.
- **Why it mattered:** Jones won at Jonesville but did **not** take Cumberland Gap. Virginia Tech's account says the surrender helped leave Lee County under Confederate control for the remainder of the war.

## A cold morning in Jonesville

Imagine Jonesville at daybreak on **Sunday, January 3, 1864**. The small courthouse town sits amid the ridges and valleys of Lee County. A Union cavalry force has spent the night there. Somewhere beyond town, Confederate mounted troops are making their approach.

The weather was bitterly cold, according to the modern Civil War Trails interpretation of the battle. The terrain that gives Lee County its beauty also made movement difficult and information uncertain: ridges could hide columns, and narrow roads could expose them at the worst possible moment.

The surprise came from the west. Confederate horsemen struck the Union camp. Artillery changed hands amid the confusion. As the day progressed, Union soldiers withdrew toward a substantial brick house and its neighboring farm buildings, seeking defensive cover. Confederate troops pressed from one side while another Confederate command approached from the east.

By late afternoon, the Union position had become untenable. The men withdrew from their protection and occupied nearby higher ground. The Confederates attacked. The surviving command surrendered.

This opening is a **narrative reconstruction of the documented sequence**, not a firsthand quotation or a claim that every sensory detail was recorded. For the underlying account, see the [National Register nomination's historical narrative, Section 8](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf) and [Virginia Tech's battle summary](https://civilwar.vt.edu/programs/drivingtour/battleofjonesville.html).

## Why Lee County and Cumberland Gap mattered

To understand why soldiers fought at Jonesville, look west to **Cumberland Gap**. The mountain pass, where Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee meet, was one of the region's historically important corridors through the Appalachians. It held strategic value because armies could use the surrounding routes to move between areas otherwise divided by difficult mountain terrain.

Throughout the Civil War, the wider borderland around eastern Tennessee, southwestern Virginia and southeastern Kentucky remained contested. Military planners watched the mountain passes, valley roads and communities that could supply or shelter troops. Jonesville stood along this wider network of routes, a relatively small town with a strategically useful location.

In late 1863, Union troops held Cumberland Gap. Confederate leaders hoped to challenge that position. A force at Jonesville could threaten Confederate movement and communicate information about approaching columns; a Confederate victory in Jonesville could, in turn, clear the approach and support an advance toward the Gap.

For a broader view of the mountain setting, explore Discover Lee VA's [Lee County heritage guide](https://discoverleeva.com/heritage) and [ultimate guide to Lee County](https://discoverleeva.com/guide-to-lee-county-virginia). Visitors interested in the region's historic travel corridors can also explore the [Cumberland Gap National Historical Park](https://www.nps.gov/cuga/index.htm).

## Southwest Virginia in the winter of 1863–1864

By the beginning of 1864, the Civil War had been raging for nearly three years. The Union and Confederacy were fighting across many fronts. In the Appalachian borderlands, fighting often took the form of cavalry movements, raids, detachments guarding roads and supply points, and engagements that did not resemble the massive battles fought elsewhere.

These mountain communities also experienced divided loyalties, requisitioning, military occupation and economic disruption. It would be inaccurate to assume every Lee County resident held the same allegiance merely because the battle ended with a Confederate victory.

In early January, Brig. Gen. William E. Jones was conducting operations directed toward Cumberland Gap. According to Virginia Tech, he crossed the Clinch River on January 2 with approximately **2,000 men**. Meanwhile, Union officers at Cumberland Gap had become aware of Confederate activity and sent a mounted force toward Jonesville.

The two movements brought opposing forces toward the same small town. The result was a clash that Jones had not originally set out to fight in quite this way.

**Research note:** Estimates of forces engaged vary by source and by whether a figure refers to a brigade's overall strength or the men immediately involved at the battlefield. The approximately 2,000 Confederates figure is from the [Virginia Tech account](https://civilwar.vt.edu/programs/drivingtour/battleofjonesville.html), not a precise count of men firing at every stage.

## The commanders and the forces

| Side | Commander / unit | Role in the engagement |
|---|---|---|
| Confederacy | Brig. Gen. William E. “Grumble” Jones | Led the Confederate movement toward Jonesville and directed the attack from the west |
| Confederacy | Lt. Col. Auburn L. Pridemore | Commanded troops operating to the east, helping close off the Union escape |
| Confederacy | 8th Virginia Cavalry and other Confederate mounted units | Took part in the battle; Lt. Col. Alphonso F. Cook submitted a separate report |
| Union | Maj. Charles H. Beeres | Commanded the Union force at Jonesville |
| Union | 16th Illinois Cavalry | Principal Union cavalry unit involved |
| Union | Detachment of the 22nd Ohio Battery | Supplied three artillery pieces involved in the fighting |
| Union | Col. Wilson C. Lemert | Commander at Cumberland Gap who sent Beeres toward Jonesville |

The [1993 National Register nomination](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf) names several Confederate units, and the [Official Records](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth152618/) contain reports from officers on both sides. The same source collection provides a way to check the sequence rather than relying solely on later retellings.

## January 1–2: The Union force enters Jonesville

The story begins before the famous January 3 attack. The National Register research states that on **January 1, 1864**, Col. Wilson C. Lemert ordered Major Beeres to occupy Jonesville. Beeres's force included more than 300 troopers associated with the 16th Illinois Cavalry and artillery from the 22nd Ohio Battery.

On **January 2**, Beeres's men pushed Confederate cavalry under Pridemore out of Jonesville. The Union troops then occupied the town and remained overnight. The nomination describes an encampment on the south side of what is now **U.S. Route 58**, across from the Dickinson-Milbourn House.

It was a location that placed soldiers close to the roads and fields around town—but also within reach of an approaching Confederate force.

Jones had been moving toward Cumberland Gap and learned that Union troops were at Jonesville. Instead of simply bypassing them, he moved to attack. The Confederates approached through the mountain country while coordinating with Pridemore's troops.

The stage was set for a fight in which the Union soldiers, having driven one Confederate force away, would face a much larger threat almost immediately.

**Sources:** [Virginia Tech, Battle of Jonesville](https://civilwar.vt.edu/programs/drivingtour/battleofjonesville.html); [Dickinson-Milbourn House National Register nomination](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf), Section 8.

## January 3: The surprise attack

At daybreak on January 3, Jones's Confederates reached Jonesville. They attacked the Union camp from the west. The surprise was substantial, though the Union troops rallied and fought back.

The day's fighting was not one uninterrupted cavalry charge. The Confederates initially seized Union artillery, but the guns were contested and the Federals managed to reestablish a defense. The Union troops shifted position as the Confederates tried to dislodge them.

A particularly valuable detail comes from the report of **Lt. Col. Alphonso F. Cook of the 8th Virginia Cavalry**, dated March 10, 1864. Cook described his regiment's role in the attack and the effort to rush the camp and artillery. His report survives in the *Official Records*, allowing readers to compare a participating officer's description with the later historical summaries.

See [Cook's report in the Official Records, Series I, Volume XXXII, Part I](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth152618/m1/81/) and the [Virginia Tech account](https://civilwar.vt.edu/programs/drivingtour/battleofjonesville.html).

### A turning point: the Union troops find shelter

Rather than disintegrating at the first attack, Beeres's men moved toward a position offering much better protection: the **Dickinson-Milbourn House** and its outbuildings. In an era of open fields, scattered buildings and limited cover, a large brick house and nearby farm structures offered defenders a meaningful advantage.

That decision would shape the remainder of the battle.

## The Dickinson-Milbourn House becomes a defensive position

![The Dickinson-Milbourn House in Jonesville, Virginia](Dickison.webp)

The Dickinson-Milbourn House is more than an attractive historic building near a battlefield. **It was part of the battlefield.** The [Virginia Department of Historic Resources](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/historic-registers/245-0004/) says Union troops sheltered within and around the house and its outbuildings during the January 3 fighting. Much of the combat and the subsequent surrender occurred on the property.

According to the National Register nomination, the Union soldiers moved to the house after the initial Confederate assault. Protected by the structures and their artillery positions, they were difficult to drive out without high losses. Jones therefore avoided an immediate costly assault and held pressure on the position while awaiting Pridemore's approach from the east.

This detail explains why the house matters. It was not merely visible from the fighting; the building and farm were part of the **tactical decisions** that prolonged the engagement.

A house built for ordinary domestic life had become the cover behind which soldiers fought for survival. The property included a number of farm outbuildings at the time, although most of those structures no longer survive.

### Did the house become a Civil War hospital?

Local tradition says the Dickinson-Milbourn House served as a hospital after the battle. The Virginia Department of Historic Resources describes that use as **probable**, and the National Register nomination discusses the tradition favorably. Still, the evidence is not the same as a surviving wartime hospital ledger or eyewitness account conclusively establishing which wounded soldiers were treated inside.

The careful, historically responsible phrasing is: **The house probably served as a hospital after the Battle of Jonesville, according to local tradition and state preservation research.**

### Why its survival is remarkable

The state register describes the building as an unusually well-preserved example of **Federal-style architecture** in far Southwest Virginia. Completed by 1848, the two-story brick dwelling retains much of its original character. Its survival links the Civil War landscape to a real structure that visitors can still see from a public street today.

The property was listed on the **Virginia Landmarks Register June 16, 1993**, and on the **National Register of Historic Places August 12, 1993** (reference **93000825**).

For architectural history, registration details, and official photographs, see [Virginia DHR's Dickinson-Milbourn House page](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/historic-registers/245-0004/) and the [complete nomination form (PDF)](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf).

## How the battle ended

For much of January 3, the Union force continued to resist. Jones's men to the west kept pressure on the position while Pridemore's Confederates moved toward Jonesville from the east.

By late in the day, the danger had changed. The Union troops were no longer facing an opponent primarily on one side. They risked being trapped between two Confederate forces.

The National Register history explains that Beeres's men left the house and outbuildings and moved onto a hill north of the dwelling. Once they were too far from the buildings to use that shelter again, Jones launched a general assault. The Union soldiers, outnumbered and surrounded, surrendered.

Jones's report describes the tactical dilemma from the Confederate perspective: he preferred to keep the enemy under pressure until their position could be attacked without the same cost in lives. His official report records the surrender and the quantity of men and equipment taken.

The outcome of the **Battle of Jonesville** was a decisive **Confederate tactical victory**, even though it did not accomplish the larger aim at Cumberland Gap.

**Primary account:** [Jones's official report, *Official Records*, p. 60](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth152618/m1/81/).

## What the historical records say about the 383 prisoners

The headline figure **383** is not a modern tourism slogan. It appears in Confederate Brig. Gen. Jones's own after-action report. He reported the following results:

| Result reported by Jones | Number |
|---|---:|
| Union officers and men captured | **383** |
| Wounded among those captured | **45** |
| Union soldiers killed | **10** |
| Artillery pieces captured | **3** |
| Six-mule wagons and teams captured | **27** |

**Important:** The **45 wounded are included within the 383 captured**; they are not an additional 45 prisoners. These numbers are Jones's reported figures, and should be cited that way rather than presented as an independently reconciled full casualty count for both armies.

The loss was serious for the Union command: hundreds of soldiers and their weapons, supplies and transport were now in Confederate hands. From a military perspective, this was far more than a small roadside skirmish.

### What happened to the captured soldiers?

The surrender made the men **prisoners of war**. Their individual experiences did not necessarily follow the same path. A precise list of which prisons each captured soldier entered, the dates of transfer, and the final fate of every prisoner requires a more detailed review of compiled service records, prisoner-of-war registers, pension files and regimental histories.

The Virginia Tech and state landmark accounts used for this article **do not establish a verified, complete destination list for all 383 prisoners**. It would therefore be misleading to claim that every soldier was sent to a particular prison or to assign a death toll without supporting rosters.

For additional primary-source research, begin with the [National Archives' Civil War records resources](https://www.archives.gov/research/military/civil-war) and the [Library of Congress Civil War collections](https://www.loc.gov/collections/civil-war-maps/about-this-collection/). The [Official Records collection](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth152618/) also contains reports by Union and Confederate officers involved in the operation.

## The Dickinson-Milbourn House before and after the war

The house's significance reaches beyond one January afternoon. Its history shows what sort of place Jonesville was before armies entered its streets.

The home was constructed **between approximately 1844 and 1848** for **Benjamin Dickinson**, a substantial Lee County landowner. Its brick construction, Federal-style design and surviving interior details made it one of the area's exceptional early dwellings.

The property later passed to **Andrew Milbourn**, who acquired it from the Dickinson heirs before the Civil War. At the time of the battle, this was a working farm with extensive lands and farm buildings, not an isolated roadside monument. This context explains the fields and outbuildings that appear so prominently in the battlefield narrative.

The 1993 nomination describes a surviving nineteenth-century **brick smokehouse**, as well as a family cemetery on the property. Visitors should not assume that every current outbuilding dates to the battle. Several later buildings were erected during the twentieth century.

After the war, the property remained associated with the Milbourn family and later owners. It eventually received formal recognition on both the state and national registers in 1993.

For those interested in nineteenth-century architecture as well as military history, the nomination is unusually worthwhile reading: it contains architectural descriptions, a chain of ownership, a history of the farm and the battle, and a careful examination of local tradition.

**Source:** [Dickinson-Milbourn House National Register nomination (1993)](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf).

## A romantic local legend—and what the records reveal

Not every remembered story about the Dickinson-Milbourn House survives scrutiny.

A local tradition held that **Henry Clay Joslyn**, who later married Andrew Milbourn's daughter **Sarah**, was a Union captain wounded and captured in the Battle of Jonesville. In the story, he recovered at the house and met the woman he would marry.

It is a compelling tale, but the National Register researchers checked the soldier's service history. Joslyn's regiment, the **29th Massachusetts**, was **not** fighting at Jonesville on January 3, 1864. The nomination places the regiment in Tennessee near Knoxville during the engagement. Joslyn later distinguished himself elsewhere in the war and attained the rank of captain, but the dramatic Jonesville meeting story is **not supported by his military record**.

The couple did marry in Lee County in **1869**. How they actually met is uncertain.

That distinction is worth preserving. Lee County's history includes both documented events and stories handed down through generations. Good historical storytelling can share both—provided readers know which is which.

**Source:** [National Register nomination, Section 8, pp. 7–8](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf).

## Why the victory did not lead to the capture of Cumberland Gap

Jonesville was a Confederate success, but the larger military objective remained out of reach.

Jones initially intended to move on **Cumberland Gap**. The fight at Jonesville consumed time and resources. His report says that his ammunition was nearly exhausted, that his supply wagons were delayed, and that he subsequently received reports showing the Union garrison at Cumberland Gap had been reinforced.

By the time Jones could realistically consider advancing, the opportunity had passed. Jones did not take Cumberland Gap, and the Union continued to hold it.

This is an important distinction when evaluating the battle: **a battlefield victory does not automatically become a successful campaign**. The capture of hundreds of Union troops mattered locally, but the Confederate force failed to secure the mountain pass that had motivated the wider operation.

Virginia Tech nevertheless notes that the surrender effectively helped leave **Lee County under Confederate control** for the remainder of the war. The consequences were therefore regional even when the broader strategic objective fell short.

**Sources:** [Jones's report in the Official Records](https://texashistory.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metapth152618/m1/81/); [Virginia Tech Civil War Studies](https://civilwar.vt.edu/programs/drivingtour/battleofjonesville.html).

## What remains of the battlefield today

**Jonesville is not an intact Civil War battlefield park.** Virginia Tech notes that the battlefield was not formally preserved as such, but several features still connect visitors to the engagement.

### 1. The Dickinson-Milbourn House

The most powerful physical connection is the house itself. Its exterior and setting help explain why Union soldiers used the property defensively. Virginia Tech places it near the corner of **Wilderness Road and Palace Street**.

**The house is privately owned and is not open for public tours.** View only from public areas, do not enter the yard or grounds, and respect residents' privacy. Historic designation does not give the public access to a private home.

### 2. The Battle of Jonesville historical marker

An interpretive **Civil War Trails** marker titled **“Battle of Jonesville — ‘We were unexpected guests’”** recounts the surprise attack and the campaign. The Historical Marker Database identifies its location around **Main Street (U.S. 58) and Richmond Street** in Jonesville.

See the [Historical Marker Database's Jonesville entries](https://www.hmdb.org/results.asp?Search=Place&State=Virginia&Town=Jonesville) for marker text, photographs and location information.

### 3. The memorial to unknown Confederate dead

A memorial to unknown Confederate soldiers killed in connection with the battle stands in a Jonesville cemetery area along **U.S. 58**, between **Old Harlan Avenue and Ely Street**, as described in Virginia Tech's driving-tour guide. It reflects later commemoration of the war and should be understood in that historical context.

### 4. Local efforts to interpret the site

In **December 2024**, the **Lee County Historical and Genealogical Society** described a project to install Civil War Trails interpretation near the Dickinson-Milbourn House and develop a battlefield driving tour with QR-code stops. The society's announcement is a valuable record of local preservation efforts; visitors should verify the current status of any planned tour materials before traveling.

Read the society's [Battle of Jonesville commemoration project announcement](https://www.leecountyvahistoricalsociety.org/post/honoring-the-battle-of-jonesville-a-2024-commemoration-project).

## Visiting Jonesville respectfully

Interested in seeing where the battle happened? Jonesville is an easy historical stop while exploring Lee County, Virginia, and the surrounding Cumberland Gap region.

**Suggested self-guided approach:** Begin with the public Civil War Trails marker in Jonesville. Read the historical account to understand the directions of the attack and the Union withdrawal. Continue along public streets to view the Dickinson-Milbourn House **from outside the property**. If accessible and appropriate, visit the roadside memorial in a respectful manner. Then explore additional local history through the town and county's visitor resources.

For a longer visit, Discover Lee VA has guides to [historic attractions](https://discoverleeva.com/heritage), [outdoor things to do](https://discoverleeva.com/outdoors), [places to stay](https://discoverleeva.com/lodging) and [Lee County visitor information](https://discoverleeva.com/visitor-info). The [Appalachian mountain road trip guide](https://discoverleeva.com/articles/appalachian-mountain-road-trip) helps connect Jonesville to the larger region.

**Visitor reminder:** The preserved home is a **private residence**, not an attraction with admission or operating hours. Historical-marker locations and access can change. Verify details before visiting and never trespass to get a closer photograph.

## FAQ

### When was the Battle of Jonesville fought?

The Battle of Jonesville occurred **January 2–3, 1864**, during the American Civil War. The principal Confederate assault and Union surrender took place **January 3**.

### Where was the Battle of Jonesville?

The battle occurred in and around **Jonesville**, the county seat of **Lee County, Virginia**, in far Southwest Virginia. Important fighting took place near the **Dickinson-Milbourn House** and surrounding fields.

### How many Union soldiers were captured at the Battle of Jonesville?

Confederate General William E. Jones reported **383 Union officers and men captured**. He said **45 of those captured were wounded**. The figure comes from his official military report.

### Who won the Battle of Jonesville?

The **Confederate forces** led by Brig. Gen. William E. “Grumble” Jones won the battle. However, they did not achieve the larger planned capture of **Cumberland Gap**.

### Who commanded the Union troops at Jonesville?

**Major Charles H. Beeres** commanded the Union detachment, which included men of the **16th Illinois Cavalry** and supporting artillery.

### What is the Dickinson-Milbourn House?

It is a historic **Federal-style brick home** built in the 1840s in Jonesville. Union soldiers used the house and its outbuildings for protection during the battle. The house is listed on the Virginia Landmarks Register and the National Register of Historic Places.

### Was the Dickinson-Milbourn House a Civil War hospital?

It **probably** served as a hospital after the battle, according to local tradition cited by Virginia historic preservation researchers. That specific use should be described as probable rather than absolutely proven.

### Can you tour the Dickinson-Milbourn House?

**No public interior tours are advertised in the official historical sources consulted.** It is a **private residence**. Visitors may appreciate its exterior from lawful public viewpoints but must respect private property.

### Is there a Battle of Jonesville monument or historical marker?

Yes. Jonesville has Civil War interpretive markers, and a memorial commemorating unknown Confederate dead associated with the battle is identified in Virginia Tech's historical driving-tour account.

### Why was Cumberland Gap important to the battle?

Jones's wider mission involved an effort to challenge Union control of Cumberland Gap, a strategically important mountain passage. After Jonesville, shortages, delay and the strengthened Union garrison prevented the Confederates from pursuing that objective.

### What happened to the 383 Union prisoners?

The men were captured and became prisoners of war. The **complete, individual post-surrender journeys of all 383 soldiers** are not documented in the main historical sources cited here; establishing them would require dedicated prisoner-roster research.

[National Register of Historic Places nomination — Dickinson-Milbourn House, 1993 (PDF)](https://www.dhr.virginia.gov/VLR_to_transfer/PDFNoms/245-0004_Dickinson-Milbourn_House_1993_Final_Nomination.pdf). **Detailed historical documentation.** Particularly valuable for the battle's chronology, the house's role, historical property records and the correction of the Henry Joslyn legend.

# The Haunting of Bertha Sybert: Lee County's Bouncing Bed Mystery of 1938

*In the winter of 1938, a nine-year-old girl in the mountains of Lee County, Virginia, became the center of a mystery that reached newspapers across the country. Witnesses said her bed shook and bounced while she lay still. Some called it witchcraft. Investigators looked for a physical explanation. More than eight decades later, the story of “Bouncing Bertha” still unsettles Appalachia.*

> **Historical note:** This is a documented **news story and local legend**, not proof of a haunting. The incidents below were *reported* by witnesses and newspapers; whether anything supernatural occurred has never been established.

## TL;DR

- **Who:** Bertha Marie Sybert, a nine-year-old Lee County girl.
- **Where:** The Sybert family home on Wallens Creek in Lee County, near Jonesville, Virginia.
- **When:** Late 1938 into early 1939.
- **What was reported:** A bed that seemed to bounce or shake, unsettling noises, and other unexplained disturbances.
- **What is verified:** Newspapers covered the story at the time, investigators visited, and local eyewitness accounts were later collected by Appalachian writer Amy C. Clark.
- **What remains unproven:** Any ghost, witchcraft, or paranormal cause.

## A winter night on Wallens Creek

Imagine an Appalachian winter evening in 1938. Beyond the windows of a mountain home on Wallens Creek, darkness settles over the ridges. Inside, a family tries to get through another night without whatever has been disturbing their sleep.

At the center of the story is a child, Bertha Sybert. Visitors have come because they have heard the rumors. They watch as she lies on a bed. Then, according to their accounts, the bed begins to move.

That opening is a reconstruction of the atmosphere, **not a transcript of one specific night**. But the central claim—the inexplicably bouncing bed—was real enough as a *reported event* to draw reporters, curious neighbors, and university investigators to Lee County. Historian and Appalachian writer [Amy C. Clark documented the recollections in *Blue Ridge Country*](https://blueridgecountry.com/archive/bouncing-bertha-sybert/).

## Who was Bertha Sybert?

Bertha Marie Sybert was born in Lee County in 1929. In late 1938, when she was nine years old, her family began reporting unusual episodes around her bed. The Syberts lived in the Wallens Creek area, in the mountains near Jonesville.

For a child living in rural Southwest Virginia during the Great Depression, the sudden attention must have been extraordinary. Instead of being known simply to her family and neighbors, Bertha became a subject of newspaper articles and public curiosity.

The [Sybert family history](https://wvancestry.com/Files/The_Sybert_Seibert_Scybert_Family_of_West_Virginia_then_Lee_County_Virginia.pdf) identifies her by name and records the attention the mysterious episodes attracted. It also places the events in late 1938 and early 1939.

## The bed that would not stay still

The most famous part of the story was disturbingly simple: Bertha would lie down, and the bed would reportedly shake or bounce beneath her.

Some visitors claimed the motion was so forceful that grown men positioned themselves around the bed in an effort to stop it. Others said the movement occurred while Bertha appeared to be lying still.

One witness, Ralph Miner, had known the family. Years later, Miner recalled seeing the episodes firsthand. His account became central to Clark's research and to the story's survival in local memory.

According to Clark's reporting, the phenomenon was also said to follow Bertha to another household, rather than remaining confined to a single room. In one widely repeated account, a bed reportedly bounced in rhythm as music was played nearby.

These details come from eyewitness recollections and period reporting. They help explain why the story terrified people, but they do **not** independently demonstrate that the bed moved without a physical cause.

## The stranger things witnesses described

The bouncing bed was only the beginning of what witnesses said they experienced.

In Clark's account, people associated with the Sybert home reported doors opening unexpectedly, wallpaper peeling, and bedding seeming to move away from visitors' hands. Bertha reportedly described seeing a vague white apparition on one occasion. Other accounts said that she became frightened of going to bed.

In a mountain community where stories of *haints* and witchcraft were familiar, it is easy to understand why some neighbors interpreted the incidents as supernatural. Bertha's grandmother was among those who reportedly suspected “witchery.”

But there is a difference between **what people believed they saw** and **what history can prove**. These are reported claims, not verified paranormal phenomena.

## When the haunting made national news

The story did not stay in Wallens Creek. As word spread, visitors and journalists came to the Sybert home, and Bertha's unusual bed became a national curiosity.

A [December 23, 1938 issue of the *Seymour Daily Tribune*](https://newspaperarchive.com/seymour-daily-tribune-dec-23-1938-p-1/) carried an Associated Press report about the mystery near Jonesville, referring to the experiments and the university professors who examined the situation.

Clark's historical feature also describes the crowds and the unwanted attention the family received. According to period coverage summarized in her article, Bertha even drew attention at a Jonesville movie theater in January 1939.

This is what sets **the Haunting of Bertha Sybert** apart from many unsourced Appalachian ghost stories: the public controversy itself is documented. People in 1938 were genuinely discussing, investigating, and writing about the case—even though they disagreed about its cause.

## What investigators concluded

Two University of Tennessee professors, Axel Brett and George Haslerud, visited to investigate. Their explanation, as described in Clark's article, pointed toward bodily muscle contractions rather than a supernatural force.

That interpretation did not persuade everyone who said they had watched the bed move. Witnesses such as Ralph Miner remained unconvinced that ordinary movement explained what they remembered.

There were skeptics outside Lee County, too. A [historical review of newspaper coverage](https://www.wildabouthoudini.com/2022/12/the-bouncing-beds-of-278.html) notes that Bess Houdini, widow of illusionist Harry Houdini, dismissed the ghostly interpretation.

The evidence supports a careful conclusion: **a much-publicized mystery occurred, but a ghost was never proved**. Historical descriptions of the investigators' work do not establish a modern scientific finding either way.

## What happened to Bouncing Bertha?

The disturbances did not continue indefinitely. A [compiled Sybert family history](https://wvancestry.com/Files/The_Sybert_Seibert_Scybert_Family_of_West_Virginia_then_Lee_County_Virginia.pdf) states that the bouncing stopped after roughly three months and did not return.

Bertha grew up and lived beyond the short period in which her childhood became a public spectacle. The same family history records that she died in 1986.

The tale, however, outlived the original reports. Decades later, Clark interviewed an eyewitness and published [*The Legend of Bouncing Bertha*](https://blueridgecountry.com/archive/bouncing-bertha-sybert/) in 2012. She revisited the story in a 2023 episode of [*The Talking Appalachian Podcast*](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2224742/episodes/13869373-the-haunting-of-bertha-sybert).

## Why this Lee County ghost story endures

Many Appalachian ghost stories begin with the words *they say*. The Bertha Sybert case has something additional: a named child, a specific part of Lee County, contemporary newspaper accounts, and memories preserved by people who said they were there.

The story also contains something more human than a typical campfire scare. At its heart was a nine-year-old girl whose family faced fear, scrutiny, and crowds of curious strangers. Whatever caused the movements, the experience became an enduring piece of Lee County folklore.

For readers drawn to [Lee County's history and heritage](https://discoverleeva.com/heritage), Bouncing Bertha is a reminder that local history includes not only roads, settlements, and historic landmarks, but also mysteries that communities continue to debate.

So what really happened in that cabin on Wallens Creek? Was it an unusual physical phenomenon, a misunderstood event, a hoax, or something nobody present could explain?

The records document the questions. They do not settle the mystery.

***And perhaps that is why, nearly ninety years later, people still remember the bed that would not stay still.***

## FAQ

### Was Bouncing Bertha a real person?

Yes. Bertha Marie Sybert was a Lee County resident born in 1929. Her identity and the widespread attention surrounding her childhood are documented in family history and newspaper accounts.

### Where did the Bouncing Bertha haunting happen?

The reports centered on the Sybert family's home in the Wallens Creek area of Lee County, Virginia, near Jonesville. The home should not be treated as an advertised public attraction.

### What year did the Bertha Sybert haunting happen?

The reports began in late 1938 and continued into early 1939.

### Did Bertha Sybert's bed really bounce?

Multiple people **reported** that it did, and newspapers published accounts at the time. Researchers also investigated. The reports are historical facts; the cause of the alleged movements—and the exact nature of what occurred—remains disputed.

### Was Bouncing Bertha a ghost or a case of witchcraft?

There is no verified evidence establishing either explanation. Investigators suggested a physical cause, while some witnesses and neighbors interpreted the events as supernatural.

### Are there verified ghost stories from Lee County, Virginia?

The Bertha Sybert case is a **verified historical news story about an alleged haunting**, not a verified ghost. The distinction matters: documents show that the story was reported and investigated, but they do not prove paranormal activity.
